# Shape notes

One or two things worth knowing about each shape: where it came from, a number, a story. Shapes are named by their geometry, as in the captions; historical names of people and places are given where they are matters of record, and legends are flagged as legends.

## Round Brilliant

The modern proportions were set out in 1919 by Marcel Tolkowsky, a 21-year-old Antwerp cutter writing a mathematics thesis in London, who worked out by hand the crown and pavilion angles that return the most light; the numbers in use today are still within a degree or two of his. Roughly three out of four diamonds sold are round brilliants, which is why every other shape is called a "fancy".

## Marquise Brilliant

Legend has it that Louis XV asked for a diamond cut to the shape of the smile of his mistress, the Marquise de Pompadour, in the 1740s; whatever the truth, the name stuck, and the French court's taste for the shape is why it is also called the navette, the little boat. Of all the shapes it has the largest face for its weight, because so much of the stone sits in the long, shallow center.

## Marquise Brilliant, Six Pavilion Mains

This version has no pavilion mains at the points: the six mains sit under the bezels along the belly, and at each end two lower girdle facets meet along the keel line, the ridge that runs down the length of the stone to the culet.

## Marquise Brilliant, Four Pavilion Mains

Here only four pavilion mains, one under each wing bezel, meet at the culet in a cross: one arm is the keel line running to the points, the other the belly line running to the sides. Fewer, larger mains tend to return broader flashes of light than the six- and eight-main layouts.

## Pear Brilliant

The pear is the oldest of the fancy brilliants: Lodewyk van Berquem of Bruges is credited with cutting the first pendeloque, a faceted teardrop, in the 1450s, soon after the invention of the scaif, the diamond-charged polishing wheel that made symmetrical facets possible. The largest polished diamond in the world, the 530-carat Great Star of Africa cut from the Cullinan in 1908, is a pear.

## Pear Brilliant, French Tip

The point is finished with a French tip, as on the marquise: small facets meeting at a hub in place of a single bezel.

## Heart Brilliant

A heart-shaped diamond is recorded as early as 1562, when Mary, Queen of Scots, sent one in a ring to Elizabeth I of England as a token of goodwill between the two queens. The shape is in effect a pear with a cleft cut into its head, often from the flat, triangular twinned crystals called macles; the cleft is cut after the main faceting, frequently by a different hand, and the symmetry of the two lobes stands or falls on that one cut landing exactly on the stone's axis.

## Oval Brilliant

Oval stones are centuries old, but the modern oval brilliant, with a round brilliant's facets stretched over an ellipse, was worked out in 1957 and quickly became a standard. Its best-known quirk is the bow-tie: a pair of dark triangles across the width, caused by the facets at the ends being flatter than those at the sides, which a careful cutter keeps faint and a careless one leaves black.

## Oval Brilliant, Four Pavilion Mains

As on the four-main marquise, four pavilion mains, one under each shoulder bezel, meet at the culet in a cross, and the lower girdle facets meet along the two axes under the head and belly bezels.

## Cushion Brilliant

The cushion is the direct descendant of the old mine cut and was the dominant diamond shape for most of the nineteenth century, before the round brilliant took over. Some of the most famous diamonds in the world are cushions, among them the 45.52-carat Hope Diamond in Washington and the 140-carat Regent in the Louvre.

## Cushion Brilliant, Open Culet

This version keeps a culet: rather than meeting at a point, the pavilion mains stop at a small flat rectangle along the length, a reminder of the large open culets of the old mine cuts the cushion came from.

## Square Cushion Modified Brilliant

The name comes from the grading report: a cushion is called a modified brilliant when it is cut with extra facets beyond the classic brilliant arrangement, usually a further row on the pavilion that breaks the cushion's broad flashes into finer sparkle, and since the line between the two is a judgment, the same stone can be called a cushion brilliant by one laboratory and a cushion modified brilliant by another. "Square" is a measurement too: IGI's cut guidelines count a cushion as square up to a length-to-width ratio of 1.05 and as rectangular from 1.06.

## Square Modified Brilliant

The square brilliant with pointed corners was developed in the 1960s and 1970s and by the end of the century had become the most popular fancy shape. It keeps up to about 80% of the weight of an octahedral rough crystal, against roughly half for a round brilliant cut from the same piece, which is why a square stone of a given size costs noticeably less than a round.

## Cut-Cornered Rectangular Modified Brilliant

Introduced in 1977, this was the first cut to put a full brilliant-style pavilion under a step-cut outline, bringing sparkle to the rectangle that had belonged to the emerald cut. Its clipped corners are practical as well as handsome: a square or rectangle with sharp corners is the most easily chipped of all shapes, and the clipped version is one of the most durable.

## Emerald Cut

The step cut was developed for emeralds, a brittle stone that cracks under the pressure needed to polish a brilliant's many small facets, and took its name from them when diamond cutters adopted it in the 1920s. Because its long facets hide nothing, it is the cut that most rewards a clean, well-colored crystal, and the shape of choice for the great Golconda diamonds of the past.

## Square Emerald Cut

The square step cut with deeply clipped corners was first cut in Amsterdam in 1902 by Joseph Asscher, and is still widely known as the Asscher cut; it became a signature of art deco jewelry in the 1920s, and a reworked version with more facets was introduced by the Asscher company in 2001 as the Royal Asscher Cut. Seen face up its facets form a stair of concentric squares, an effect sometimes called the hall of mirrors or the windmill.

## Shield Brilliant

The shield has been cut for centuries as a way of saving a crystal whose outline would not yield a round or a pear, which is why no two are quite alike and no laboratory publishes a template for it. It is most often seen as a pair of side stones flanking a larger center, pointing in toward it, though a well-cut shield holds its own as a center stone.

## Kite Shield, Hexagonal Shield and Wide Shield, Step Cut

Six-sided step-cut shields are cut to the stone's own outline rather than to a standard, so proportions vary from one to the next; the three drawn here were measured from photographs of cut stones. The flat facets of a step cut show color and clarity plainly, which makes these shapes a favorite for colored stones and for the salt-and-pepper diamonds that have come into fashion.

## Triangular Brilliant

The triangular brilliant was first cut in Amsterdam in 1962, and a New York firm registered a trademark on a version in 1978, which is why the shape goes by several different names in the trade. It is one of the shallowest of all cuts, often only a third as deep as it is wide, so a triangular stone looks far larger face up than its weight suggests.

## Single Cut

The single cut, also called the eight cut, has only seventeen or eighteen facets and is the way the very smallest diamonds have been cut for well over a century, because a full brilliant's fifty-seven facets would be too tiny to polish or to see on a stone a millimeter across. Most of the sparkle in pavé and in watch bezels comes from single cuts, and good ones are still prized by setters for their crisp, even flashes.

## Old Mine Cut

The old mine cut is the brilliant of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, cut by hand and eye to the shape of each crystal; the name arose after the South African discoveries of the 1870s, when stones from the "old mines" of India and Brazil needed a name to tell them apart. Its high crown, small table and open culet were made to glitter by candlelight, and antique stones in this cut are now sought after precisely because no two are alike.
